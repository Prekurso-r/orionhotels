The house

A station for looking up, that happens to have beds.

The observatory came first. In 1931 a Belgian cartographer named Hélène Roux hauled a nine-inch refractor up the Camino del Alba because the air here holds almost no water, and water is what blurs a star. She built four rooms beside the dome so her assistants would not have to descend at dawn.

Those four rooms are now forty-two. The telescope still works. Everything we have added since — the kitchen, the baths cut into the rock, the long room with its brass and its silence — was added on one condition: that it never spills light onto the sky.

The dome

Nine inches of Belgian glass, and no queue.

Roux's refractor is still mounted on its original pier, now driven by a motor instead of a hand crank. Guests book it the way they would book a table: by the hour, from nine until the small hours, with a resident astronomer if you want one and nobody at all if you don't.

The days

What there is to do, and not do.

Six things we are good at. Everything else, we will happily arrange and quietly stay out of.

All experiences
01

Night sessions

An hour at the refractor, or four. Deep sky in winter, planets in summer, and a thermos of hot chocolate either way.

02

The salt flats

Out at 05:20 in a canvas-topped Land Cruiser, back by ten with flamingos seen and breakfast eaten on a folding table.

03

Table Nine

Twelve seats facing the pass. A tasting menu written each morning against whatever the altiplano growers brought up.

04

The rock baths

Three pools cut into the hillside at 38, 34 and 12 degrees, fed by the same spring that supplies the kitchen.

05

Silent hours

From 23:00 the house lights drop to amber and stay there. No music, no screens in the public rooms. It is the best thing we do.

06

The long walk

Eleven kilometres along the old mule track to the abandoned nitrate station, with a guide who knows where the water is.
