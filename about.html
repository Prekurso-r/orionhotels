Our story

We are not a hotel with a telescope. We are a telescope that learned to make breakfast.

Hélène Roux came to the Atacama in 1930 with a theodolite, a letter of credit and a conviction that the driest place on earth would also be the clearest. She was right. The plateau above the Camino del Alba holds a median seeing of six-tenths of an arcsecond — a number that means nothing to most guests and everything to the ones who ask.

She built the dome first and the rooms second, and only because her assistants kept falling asleep on the floor. Four rooms. A stove. A well that still supplies the kitchen and the baths. When Hélène died in 1968 her daughter Ana inherited a working observatory and an accidental inn, and made the decision the family has been living inside ever since: keep both, and let neither ruin the other.

That is why there are no floodlights on the drive, why the pool is unlit after eleven, why every exterior fixture is shielded and amber, and why the last renovation took four years instead of one. It is an expensive way to run a hotel. It is the only way to run this one.

The record

Ninety-five years, abbreviated.

1931

The dome

Hélène Roux raises a nine-inch Zeiss refractor at 2,410 metres and builds four rooms beside it so her assistants need not descend at dawn.

1948

The first paying guest

A mining engineer asks to stay the night and leaves money on the table. The ledger from that week is still in the long room, under glass.

1972

The well and the baths

Ana Roux cuts three pools into the hillside and pipes them from the same spring that feeds the kitchen. The water arrives at 38 degrees and has never been heated.

2006

Forty-two

The east wing opens. Every window faces away from the valley road, and every exterior fixture in the house is replaced with a shielded amber fitting.

2019

Table Nine

The kitchen is rebuilt around twelve seats and a pass facing the pass. Two stars follow within four years, which surprised nobody here more than the chef.

2024

Dark Sky Sanctuary

The plateau is certified Bortle Class 1 — the darkest measurable category. The nearest artificial light of any consequence is 94 kilometres away.

House rules

Three things we will not trade.

Everything else is negotiable, and usually is. These are not.

01

The dark

No uplighting, no lit signage, no illuminated pool after eleven. Every fitting outside is shielded and under 2200 K. If a fixture would put light above the horizontal, it does not get installed.

02

The water

One spring feeds the kitchen, the baths and the garden. It has never been supplemented and never will be, which is the real reason there are forty-two rooms and not ninety.

03

The quiet

No music in the public rooms, ever. No screens. From 23:00 the house drops to amber and the staff stop talking above a murmur. Guests find this alarming for one night and irreplaceable thereafter.

Hélène Roux, 1954

Anyone can build a room. What is difficult, and what is worth money, is building a room that does not interrupt anything.

Who is here

Thirty-one people, most of them local.

The average tenure at Orion is nine years. Two of the housekeeping team are the granddaughters of Hélène's original assistants.

Camila Roux

Director · third generation

Took the house over in 2016 having sworn for two decades that she never would. Answers the reservations line herself most mornings.

Mateo Alfaro

Head chef · Table Nine

Cooks against the altiplano growing calendar and refuses to write a menu more than twelve hours ahead. Twelve seats, one sitting, no substitutions.

Dr Tomás Vidal

Resident astronomer

Nineteen years on the mountain. Will talk about the Orion Nebula for four hours if allowed, and knows exactly when to stop and let you look.
